## 2. Materials and Methods (data processing and statistics)

### 2.x Study design of the independent cohort
First-morning mid-stream urine samples were collected from [n] patients with bladder cancer (BC), [n] patients with prostate cancer (PC) and [n] controls [collection site/period, ethics approval as in ABC or new approval number]. One patient with a benign bladder lesion and PC was assigned to the PC group; one patient with multiple primary tumours (PC, BC and skin cancer) and patients with kidney (n = 3) or pancreatic (n = 2) cancer were excluded. The final analysis set comprised 45 controls, 29 BC and 20 PC patients (Table 1); age was missing for four controls and sex for two controls. Group membership, sex, age and exclusions were read from a single metadata file; no sample subsets were defined manually.

### 2.x LC-HRMS analysis and feature extraction
Samples were prepared and analysed with the DoE-optimised protocol and the LC-HRMS method described in [ABC] (Acquity UPLC BEH C18, 23-min gradient, Orbitrap Fusion Lumos, ESI+, ddMS2). Pooled QC samples (n = 6) were injected at the start of the sequence and after every 20 study samples.

Raw data were processed in MS-DIAL (v. 5.5) for peak detection, deconvolution and alignment. Feature detection used a minimum peak height of 10,000 counts and a mass slice width of 0.1 Da. Peaks were smoothed with a Savitzky–Golay filter (5 scans), with a minimum peak width of 5 scans. Deconvolution used a sigma window of 0.5 and an amplitude cut-off of 100 counts. Alignment used a representative QC sample as reference, with retention-time and m/z tolerances of 0.2 min and 0.015 Da (retention-time and m/z factors 0.5/0.5). Adducts were annotated with the default ESI+ adduct and dimer library, supplemented with sodium formate clusters. Features with a sample-to-blank fold change < 5 were removed. [Authors: keep ONE of the two sentences, after checking the MS-DIAL project (Data normalization → "LOWESS" / "QC-based" option) for this batch: (A) "A QC-based LOWESS signal correction was applied within MS-DIAL." or (B) "No QC-based signal correction was applied in MS-DIAL."] MS/MS spectra were matched against NIST 23 HRMS and the MS-DIAL metabolomics MSP spectral kit, with additional annotation in MS-FINDER (v. 3.6).

The exported feature table was processed further in Python as described below. The blank filter had already been applied in MS-DIAL, so blank injections are not part of the exported table.

### 2.x Data pre-processing
Zero intensities were treated as missing. Features were retained when detected in ≥ 80% of QC injections and in ≥ 50% of samples of at least one group (14,693 → 8,867 features) and when the relative standard deviation in QC samples was ≤ 30% (→ 7,403 features). No drift correction was applied in the Python pipeline. Leave-one-QC-out prediction error was 0.081 log2 units without correction and 0.074 with LOWESS; with six QC injections such a correction cannot be validated independently. All QC RSD values refer to the exported MS-DIAL table without any correction in the pipeline [if (A): "i.e., after the MS-DIAL QC correction"] (median 10.4% for features passing the detection filter and 8.6% after the RSD filter). Urine dilution was corrected by probabilistic quotient normalisation (PQN; reference = median QC spectrum); MSTUS normalisation (1,900 features detected in all samples) was computed in parallel as a sensitivity analysis. Missing values (11.8%) were replaced by half of the feature minimum and data were log2-transformed. The fraction of imputed values per group is reported for every feature; features detected in < 50% of one group and ≥ 80% of another (n = 41) were analysed separately as presence/absence features (Fisher's exact test). Seven samples with a PQN factor < 0.2 were flagged for chromatogram inspection and analysed in a sensitivity analysis without them.

### 2.x Grouping of features into compounds
Features eluting within 0.02 min and correlated across samples (Spearman ρ ≥ 0.8) were grouped into compounds (connected components); adduct, isotope and in-source-fragment mass differences were annotated. The 7,403 features formed 5,792 compounds (718 with ≥ 2 ions). Statistics and false-discovery-rate (FDR) control were applied at the compound level using the most intense ion as representative.

### 2.x Contamination index
A homologous series of alkyl diethanolamines and their oxygenated/ethoxylated analogues ([M+H]+, mass increments C2H4 28.031, O 15.995 and C2H4O 44.026; ten features, mass error < 1.5 ppm) was identified as a plastic-derived surfactant contamination. A contamination index was defined as the mean z-score of these ten features and was used as a continuous covariate and dichotomised at the median (high/low). A co-varying unknown C24H30O6 ([M+H]+ and [M+Na]+) is reported separately and is not part of the index.

### 2.x Exposure-related compounds
Compounds were labelled as exposure-related when (i) they belonged to the plastic-surfactant series (contamination index), (ii) a manually curated annotation (Table S4) classified them as drug or food constituents, or (iii) their MS-DIAL library name matched a pre-specified keyword list (curation/exposure_keywords.csv, 21 regular expressions: drugs commonly prescribed to urological and elderly patients, grouped by therapeutic class; dietary methylxanthines, polyphenols, alkaloids and other food constituents; nicotine and its metabolites). The list was compiled by the authors before the statistical analysis; no external database was queried automatically, and the flags therefore depend on the library names returned by MS-DIAL (NIST 23 HRMS, MS-DIAL MSP). Low-score library matches were reported as "unknown" in figures (putative names only in the Supplementary Tables). Exposure-related compounds could not qualify as findings and were excluded from classification models and pathway analysis.

### 2.x Statistical analysis
For each comparison (BC vs Control, primary; BC vs PC; PC vs Control; PC vs male controls) the main model was a linear model of log2 intensity with group, sex, age and contamination index as covariates (complete cases), with Benjamini–Hochberg FDR. Robustness was assessed by (i) 1:1 nearest-age matching within sex (caliper ± 5 years, optimal assignment; Mann–Whitney U test on matched sets), (ii) comparison with controls of low contamination index, and (iii) comparison of controls with high vs low contamination index. A compound was considered a **finding** when q < 0.05 in the main model, p < 0.05 in the age-matched comparison in the same direction, p < 0.05 versus low-contamination controls in the same direction, and p ≥ 0.05 between high- and low-contamination controls; exposure-related compounds were excluded.

Classification was evaluated by 20 × 5-fold stratified cross-validation. Before cross-validation, exposure-related compounds and compounds correlated with the contamination index (|ρ| ≥ 0.5) were removed from the candidate pool (5,351 compounds remained). Within each training fold the five compounds with the smallest Mann–Whitney p were selected and a logistic regression was fitted; PLS-DA (two components, Pareto scaling within folds) on all pool compounds was used for comparison. A third model residualised all features on the contamination index within each training fold. Significance was assessed by 1,000 label permutations. Single-covariate baseline models (sex only; contamination index only; logistic regression on the covariate) were evaluated with exactly the same repeated cross-validation and permutation test. Age-matched cross-validation was performed only with ≥ 10 pairs. As a post hoc sensitivity analysis, cases and references were also matched 1:1 within sex on the contamination index (optimal assignment, caliper 0.25 SD of the index), so that contamination was balanced by design rather than by statistical adjustment.

Pathway enrichment (mummichog, positive mode, 5 ppm; top 10% of features ranked by the main-model p; exposure-related features excluded; 500 permutations; BH correction across pathways) is reported in the Supplementary Material only.

### 2.x Comparison with stage-1 markers
The 14 metabolites reported as altered in BC in stage 1 ([ABC], Table 5) were searched in the full, unfiltered feature table within ± 5 ppm of the reported m/z or of the theoretical m/z calculated from the formula and ion, and within ± 0.5 min of the reported retention time [authors: justify the window with the retention shift between stages for compounds identified by MS/MS in both datasets — median, IQR and range; see README "Сдвиг RT"]. Both stages used the same column and gradient [authors: confirm]. All candidate features within the window were reported (isomers flagged). For Leu-Leu-Tyr and Leu-Thr-Ser the formula of the named peptide was used, because the formulas listed in [ABC] Table 5 do not correspond to these peptides. Replication criteria, defined before the analysis, were: same direction and Mann–Whitney p < 0.05 (nominal) and same direction with q < 0.05 in the adjusted model (replicated).

### 2.x Annotation confidence
Annotation levels follow the Metabolomics Standards Initiative, consistent with [ABC]: level 2 for a precursor within 5 ppm of the curated formula and an MS/MS library match flagged by MS-DIAL (NIST 23 HRMS / MS-DIAL MSP) for the same feature; isomers that the MS/MS spectrum does not distinguish (e.g., Leu-Val, Val-Ile and Ile-Val for Val-Leu; sn-1/sn-2 regioisomers of lysophosphatidylcholines) are listed with each compound; level 3 for a consistent formula without MS/MS confirmation, for MS/MS matches to several isomers, or for MS/MS matches not verified against a curated formula; level 4 for all other features, for annotations with a mass error > 5 ppm and for library matches judged implausible in human urine (e.g., ethyl 2-amino-5-methyl-3-thiophenecarboxylate, a synthetic building block matched to an in-source fragment). No reference standards were analysed (no level 1). The same rule and the same source of MS/MS flags were used for Table 2, Figures 3–4 and Table S4.

### 2.x Software and code availability
Raw data were processed in MS-DIAL 5.5 and MS-FINDER 3.6. Downstream processing used Python 3.13 with numpy 2.5, pandas 3.0, scipy 1.18, scikit-learn 1.9, statsmodels 0.15, matplotlib 3.11, seaborn 0.13, joblib 1.6, adjustText 1.4 and mummichog 2.7 (exact versions in requirements.txt). Ions were grouped into compounds when they eluted within 0.02 min and were correlated across samples with Spearman ρ ≥ 0.8 (connected components; the most intense ion represented the compound). Age-matched pairs were formed 1:1 within sex by optimal assignment (Hungarian algorithm) on absolute age difference with a caliper of ± 5 years; participants without age or sex were not matched. All random steps (cross-validation splits, permutations, bootstrap, mummichog permutations) were seeded from a single fixed seed (20261013). Exposure flags were assigned by the rules described in Section 2.x (Exposure-related compounds). All parameters are set in one configuration file, and a single command (python run_all.py) regenerates every table and figure from the MS-DIAL feature table and the sample metadata file.

## 3. Results

### 3.1 Data quality
QC samples clustered tightly in PCA (Figure 1c) and total ion intensity of QC injections was stable over the sequence (Figure 1a). After filtering, 7,403 features (5,792 compounds) with median QC RSD 8.6% were retained (Figure 1b).

### 3.2 Cohort characteristics
Age was available for 41/45 controls and all BC and PC patients (Table 1). The groups differed in age (Kruskal–Wallis p = 2·10⁻⁶; BC 62.9 ± 9.7 vs Control 53.3 ± 13.9 years, p = 0.006) and sex (BC 24/5 vs Control 18/25 male/female, Fisher p = 6·10⁻⁴). Nearest-age matching within sex yielded 18 BC–Control pairs (13 male, 5 female), 18 BC–PC pairs and 9 PC–male-control pairs (Figure S2).

### 3.3 Plastic-derived contamination
The contamination index was highest in PC (mean +0.87 vs −0.37 in controls and −0.02 in BC; Figure 2). As a single variable in the same cross-validation it discriminated PC from controls with AUC 0.86 and from male controls with AUC 0.93, and BC from PC with AUC 0.78. The index was inversely related to the PQN dilution factor (Spearman ρ = −0.45 overall; about −0.6 within controls and within PC), i.e., it partly reflects urine concentration: a fixed amount of leached surfactant appears larger after normalisation of dilute urine.

### 3.4 Bladder cancer vs controls
In the model adjusted for sex, age and contamination (29 BC vs 39 controls), 295 compounds had q < 0.05; 212 of them also met the age-matched and contamination robustness criteria (117 higher and 95 lower in BC; Figures 3a, 4, S1; Table 2 for annotated findings, Table S1 for all). Annotation confidence was limited (Table 2): four findings reached MSI level 2 (precursor within 5 ppm and MS/MS library match) — dodecanoylcarnitine, biliverdin, LysoPC(16:0) and Val-Leu (isomers listed in Table 2); three were level 3 — a C19H28O3 oxo-androstane steroid (putatively 11-oxoetiocholanolone; [M+H−H2O]+ and [M+H−2H2O]+ at the same retention time; q = 0.006, age-matched p < 0.001) whose MS/MS matched two isomeric library entries, and two isomeric tetrahydrocortisone glucuronides (C27H40O11; [M+H]+ 541.2642 at 11.06 min and [M+NH4]+ 558.2914 at 10.76 min, each co-eluting with aglycone ions whose MS/MS matched tetrahydrocortisone); the remaining 205 were level 4, including one library match judged implausible (ethyl 2-amino-5-methyl-3-thiophenecarboxylate). All findings are therefore reported as candidates for confirmation, not as a diagnostic panel.

In cross-validation the five-compound panel reached AUC 0.81 ± 0.03 (permutation p < 0.001) on all samples and 0.78 ± 0.05 (p = 0.002) after in-fold adjustment for contamination; the single-covariate baselines, evaluated with the same cross-validation, reached 0.65 ± 0.02 for sex (p < 0.001) and 0.59 ± 0.02 for the contamination index (p = 0.087) (Figure 5a). In the 18 age- and sex-matched pairs the panel reached AUC 0.71 ± 0.05 (p = 0.059) and 0.58 after contamination adjustment (p = 0.23), while the contamination index alone gave 0.68 (p = 0.037) in this subset. In 15 pairs matched within sex on the contamination index (index alone: AUC 0.32, i.e., no discrimination), the panel reached 0.71 ± 0.07 (p = 0.073). The matched estimates are consistent (≈ 0.71) but, with 15–18 pairs, do not reach significance; discrimination independent of age, sex and contamination is therefore not established.

### 3.5 Bladder vs prostate cancer
Three compounds met all criteria, all lower in BC and unannotated (MSI 4; m/z 295.2132 @ 1.80 min, 411.2498 @ 8.02 min, 403.1604 @ 11.18 min; the last detected in 10% of BC vs 80% of PC samples). The panel AUC of 0.86 ± 0.04 decreased to 0.63 ± 0.07 (permutation p = 0.11) after in-fold adjustment for contamination (age-matched, 18 pairs: 0.78 → 0.61, p = 0.18), while the contamination index alone gave AUC 0.78 ± 0.02 (Figure 5b). In 13 pairs matched within sex on the contamination index, the panel reached only 0.57 ± 0.06 (p = 0.28). The separation of BC and PC is therefore attributed mainly to contamination rather than to tumour-specific metabolism.

### 3.6 Prostate cancer vs controls
No compound reached q < 0.05 after adjustment for sex, age and contamination. After removal of contamination-correlated compounds the panel did not discriminate PC from controls (AUC 0.54 ± 0.06, p = 0.35; Figure 5c), whereas the contamination index alone gave 0.86 ± 0.00 and sex alone 0.75 ± 0.01. With nine age-matched and five contamination-matched male pairs, matched models were not estimated (< 10 pairs).

After in-fold residualisation on the contamination index the panel AUC rose to 0.74 ± 0.07 (p = 0.012). We examined this increase for leakage. The residualisation coefficients are estimated in the training fold only and applied unchanged to the test fold; the procedure gave AUC 0.45 when all features were replaced by random noise and 0.58 when the index values were permuted across samples, so it does not create signal by itself. The increase arises only with the true index, which almost separates PC from controls (AUC 0.86). When a covariate nearly separates the groups, the "adjusted" group difference of any feature that co-varies with the covariate is an extrapolation of its slope across the gap between the groups, and part of the covariate's group difference is transferred into the adjusted features. Three observations show that this, rather than PC-specific metabolism, explains the increase: (i) in the sex-homogeneous comparison of PC with male controls the adjustment did not increase the AUC (0.62 → 0.61); (ii) estimating the slope within groups (ANCOVA-type adjustment) increased the AUC further, to 0.85, i.e., to the level of the contamination index alone; and (iii) no compound reached q < 0.05 in the adjusted univariate model. The adjusted AUC for PC vs Control is therefore not interpreted as evidence of a contamination-independent PC signal.

### 3.7 Comparison with stage-1 markers
Of the 14 stage-1 markers, 7 were not detected within the m/z and retention-time windows, 2 were detected below the detection filter (Leu-Leu-Arg, 2-iminobiotin) and 5 were quantified (Figure 6, Table S3). 5-Hydroxyomeprazole was found only at its theoretical m/z (362.1171, +0.5 ppm; ΔRT −0.06 min), indicating that the m/z listed in [ABC] Table 5 (362.1248) is +22 ppm in error; it did not differ between groups (p = 0.84). Val-Leu was replicated: of two isomeric candidates, the feature annotated as Val-Leu (3.72 min; MSI 2) was higher in BC (log2FC 1.99, AUC 0.76, p = 1.4·10⁻⁴), remained significant in the adjusted model (q = 0.048) and in the age-matched comparison (p = 0.004). Glutaminylproline and LysoPE(20:4) changed in the same direction without significance, and Leu-Thr-Ser changed in the opposite direction (log2FC −1.03, p = 0.035). Thus one of 14 stage-1 markers was replicated. ### 3.8 Sensitivity analyses
PQN and MSTUS differed only by a per-sample scaling: compound fold changes were perfectly rank-correlated (ρ = 1.00); MSTUS yielded 203 significant compounds (all within the 295 PQN set) and 125 findings (120 shared), with similar panel AUC (0.80 vs 0.81; Figure S3). Cross-validation results for all models are given in Table S2. The seven samples with PQN factor < 0.2 (3 BC, 3 PC, 1 control) were retained in the main analysis. Excluding them increased the number of significant compounds in BC vs Control from 295 to 490 (277 shared) and the number of findings from 212 to 371 (190 of the 212 retained), and left the cross-validated panel AUC unchanged (0.80 vs 0.81). No pathway reached FDR < 0.05 (Figure S4).
